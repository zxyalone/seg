# 2. Related Work

## 2.1 Liver tumor segmentation

**3D baselines and pretraining.** H-DenseUNet combined a 2D DenseUNet for intra-slice features with a 3D network that aggregates inter-slice context, and was evaluated on LiTS and 3DIRCADb \cite{li2018hdenseunet}. nnU-Net automates preprocessing, network topology, training and post-processing from dataset properties \cite{isensee2021nnunet}. Its 3D variant won the LiTS evaluation at MICCAI 2018 \cite{bilic2023lits}. Swin UNETR uses a hierarchical Swin Transformer encoder with a convolutional decoder \cite{hatamizadeh2022swinunetr}. MedNeXt scales ConvNeXt-style blocks with large kernels to 3D \cite{roy2023mednext}. U-Mamba inserts state-space (Mamba) blocks into an nnU-Net-based network \cite{ma2024umamba}. MambaClinix combines hierarchical gated convolutions with Mamba stages and a region-specific Tversky loss inside nnU-Net, and retrains all baselines in that framework \cite{bian2026mambaclinix}. nnU-Net Revisited re-benchmarked CNN-, Transformer- and Mamba-based models under controlled conditions \cite{isensee2024revisited}. Well-configured CNN U-Nets within nnU-Net remained strongest, and many claimed gains traced back to weak baselines. VoCo learns 3D representations by predicting the contextual position of random crops relative to base crops \cite{wu2024voco}. Its large-scale extension adds omni-supervised pretraining on 160K CT volumes, including a pseudo-labeled subset (VoComni) with 20 organ and tumor classes \cite{wu2024largescale}.

**2D and 2.5D networks.** Most of the recent liver-tumor networks cited here operate on slices. AD-DUNet pairs an axial-Transformer encoder with a cascaded dilated-convolution encoder and trains without pretraining \cite{qi2024addunet}. TCA-Net (2.5D) combines multi-core pooling, atrous spatial pyramid pooling, residual skip fusion and attention \cite{wu2025tcanet}. RIOUNeXt uses omni-dimensional dynamic convolutions and iterative attentional feature fusion \cite{wang2025rioUNeXt}. T-MPEDNet adds Transformer-inspired attention and morphological boundary refinement to a progressive encoder–decoder \cite{raghaw2025tmpednet}. Spider-Net uses a high-resolution multi-scale attention encoder and a branch that rejects benign look-alikes \cite{peng2024spidernet}. BPP-Net uses parallel antagonistic Mamba streams to target low-contrast regions with 0.14M parameters \cite{zhu2025bppnet}.

**Boundary- and instance-aware methods.** EG-UNETR applies a Canny edge detector to coarse predictions and feeds the result to an edge-guided attention block \cite{cheng2024egunetr}. PGC-Net reasons jointly over region and contour graphs \cite{you2024pgcnet}. MSML-AttUNet predicts multi-scale tumor boundaries as an auxiliary task \cite{hu2024msml}. ABeDice modifies the Dice loss to sharpen boundaries; it was validated on non-CT 2D datasets \cite{zheng2025abedice}. For small lesions, OKMIL performs box-supervised multiple-instance learning \cite{liu2025okmil}, and blob loss adds an instance-level term so that large lesions cannot dominate the Dice of small ones \cite{kofler2023blob}.

**Position of PhASER-3D.** In the methods above, boundary information comes from intensity or prediction gradients, or from learned boundary heads. These cues inherit the lesion's contrast or the network's current confidence. PhASER-3D instead adds a contrast-normalized structure measure at the feature level. It is built on the nnU-Net pipeline with a VoCo-pretrained encoder, so any gain is measured against a strong 3D baseline \cite{isensee2024revisited}.

## 2.2 Frequency- and wavelet-domain segmentation networks

WaveCNet replaces pooling and strided convolution with discrete wavelet transform (DWT) layers \cite{li2020wavecnn}. It keeps the low-frequency sub-band and drops the high-frequency sub-bands to improve noise robustness in image classification. The band selection is fixed by design.

MSFFE-Net is the closest liver-tumor work \cite{ma2025msffe}. Its spatial–frequency domain fusion (SFDF) step computes a 2D fast Fourier transform (FFT) of the whole slice. It min–max normalizes the centered magnitude spectrum to [0, 255] and concatenates it with the CT slice as a fourth input channel. A dual-branch 2D encoder with attention-refined skips follows. PFCR differs in three respects. (i) The fourth MSFFE-Net channel is indexed by spatial frequency, not by image position, so its pixels are not co-located with the CT pixels beside it; PFCR computes PC per voxel. (ii) The magnitude spectrum discards phase, which carries edge location; PFCR uses phase. (iii) MSFFE-Net injects frequency information once, at the input of a 2D network; PFCR acts on 3D encoder features at five resolutions and maps its result back to the spatial domain by an inverse Haar transform.

TexLiverNet adds an adaptive frequency filter to 2D skip connections \cite{jiang2025texlivernet}. It applies an FFT to coarse high-frequency features, multiplies the spectrum by the output of a two-layer learned filter, and returns to the spatial domain; it also fuses lesion-specific text. The filter adapts to each feature map, but the same spectral gain applies at every spatial position. The foreground-aware spectrum segmentation (FASS) framework of Han et al. targets low-contrast abdominal tumors with a 3D U-Net backbone \cite{han2025frequency}. Its feature-level module decomposes encoder features with a wavelet transform, cross-attends between detail sub-bands, and reconstructs with the inverse transform, alongside foreground-aware and edge-constraint modules.

PhASER-3D shares the wavelet decomposition and inverse reconstruction with FASS and WaveCNet but differs in what controls the band weights: a router predicts low/mid/high weights at each voxel from PC and a tumor prior. To our knowledge, none of the cited frequency modules conditions per-voxel band weighting on a contrast-invariant measure of local structure.

## 2.3 Phase congruency and the monogenic signal

Kovesi defined phase congruency as the degree to which the Fourier components of a signal are in phase at a point \cite{kovesi1999phase}. Step edges, lines and Mach bands all produce high PC. PC is dimensionless and invariant to brightness and contrast, which motivated universal thresholds across images. Kovesi computed PC with log-Gabor quadrature filters, weighted it by frequency spread for localization, and compensated for noise by subtracting an energy threshold estimated from the finest-scale response, assuming additive noise with a flat spectrum.

Felsberg and Sommer introduced the monogenic signal, an isotropic generalization of the analytic signal based on the Riesz transform \cite{felsberg2001monogenic}. It yields local amplitude, phase and orientation and preserves the split of identity between energetic and structural information. The paper develops the 2D case, but the Riesz transform is defined in any dimension, so the construction extends to 3D volumes without sampling filter orientations.

FSIM used PC, computed in Kovesi's ratio form, as its primary feature for image quality assessment \cite{zhang2011fsim}. Because PC is contrast-invariant, FSIM adds gradient magnitude as a secondary feature to encode contrast. This phase/amplitude complementarity motivates our gradient-gate control (A1, Section 4.6).

PhASER-3D adopts the monogenic formulation, the PC ratio form and Kovesi's threshold, makes the log-Gabor center wavelengths, bandwidths and noise multiplier learnable, and computes PC on a learnable one-channel projection of network features rather than on intensities. Kovesi's threshold is expressed in the units of the filter responses, whereas PC itself is not. Section 4.8 examines what this means for low-contrast lesions under CT noise.

## 2.4 Evaluation protocols on LiTS

LiTS distributes 131 training volumes and keeps 70 test volumes hidden. Its challenge ranking used Dice computed per case and averaged over cases \cite{bilic2023lits}. The benchmark paper also defined lesion-wise precision, recall and F1. The same 131 volumes form MSD Task 03 \cite{simpson2019msd}. Because test labels are hidden, most studies split the 131 volumes themselves. Table S1 summarizes the protocols of the papers in Section 2.1. Three sources of heterogeneity dominate.

1. **Split unit.** MSFFE-Net (89.61%), AD-DUNet (89.45%) and MSML-AttUNet (87.74%) split at the slice level \cite{ma2025msffe,qi2024addunet,hu2024msml}. EG-UNETR (84.45%) and BPP-Net (82.06%) use only tumor-containing slices \cite{cheng2024egunetr,zhu2025bppnet}. T-MPEDNet (89.1%) does not state its split unit \cite{raghaw2025tmpednet}. Slice-level splits place adjacent slices of one patient in both training and test sets. Patient-level studies report lower values: Spider-Net 85.94% and TCA-Net 83.90% (2D/2.5D), RIOUNeXt 76.83%, PGC-Net 73.63% (per case), MambaClinix 71.37% (3D), and OKMIL 65.22% (weakly supervised) \cite{peng2024spidernet,wu2025tcanet,wang2025rioUNeXt,you2024pgcnet,bian2026mambaclinix,liu2025okmil}.
2. **Aggregation.** On the same model and test set, PGC-Net reports a global Dice of 85.72% and a per-case Dice of 73.63% \cite{you2024pgcnet}. Most papers do not state which aggregation they use.
3. **Baseline training.** Reported nnU-Net tumor Dice ranges from 66.57% \cite{bian2026mambaclinix} to 89.54% \cite{ma2025msffe} across papers. Under the slice-level protocol of MSFFE-Net, the proposed network exceeds nnU-Net by 0.07 points.

External validation adds a further issue. LiTS training data include 20 scans from IRCAD and no IRCAD test scans \cite{bilic2023lits}. These appear to be the 20 volumes of 3DIRCADb-01, so a model trained on all LiTS training data and tested on 3DIRCADb has likely seen the test patients. In the one cited study that trained on LiTS and tested directly on 3D-IRCADb, tumor Dice fell from 84.45% to 45.40% \cite{cheng2024egunetr}.

Metrics Reloaded recommends choosing metrics from task properties, including instance-level metrics when individual lesions matter \cite{maierhein2024metrics}. Its companion paper catalogs pitfalls such as aggregation choices and the sensitivity of overlap metrics to small structures \cite{reinke2024pitfalls}. Following these recommendations and \cite{isensee2024revisited}, we retrain all compared methods in one pipeline on a patient-level split. We report per-case and global Dice together with lesion-wise and stratified metrics, and we use a TCIA cohort rather than 3DIRCADb for external testing (Section 4.3).
