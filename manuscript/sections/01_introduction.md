# 1. Introduction

## 1.1 Clinical problem and challenges

Contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) is the standard imaging modality for detecting and staging liver tumors. Voxel-level tumor delineation supports volumetry, resection and ablation planning, and response assessment. Manual delineation is slow and varies between readers, so automatic segmentation has been studied for more than a decade. The Liver Tumor Segmentation benchmark (LiTS) \cite{bilic2023lits} made this problem measurable. It provides 131 training volumes from seven centers and has also served as Task 03 of the Medical Segmentation Decathlon (MSD) \cite{simpson2019msd}.

Liver segmentation on LiTS is close to solved: the best per-case liver Dice reached 0.963. Tumor segmentation is not. The best per-case tumor Dice in the three LiTS challenge editions was 0.674, 0.702 and 0.739 \cite{bilic2023lits}. The benchmark authors also found that strong segmentation methods are not necessarily strong lesion detectors, and that small lesions were the hardest to find.

Two properties of the data drive this gap. First, lesions vary widely in size, number, shape and enhancement pattern. Second, many lesions differ only slightly from the surrounding parenchyma. We quantify this with the lesion–liver contrast-to-noise ratio (CNR), defined as the absolute difference between the mean lesion intensity and the mean of a ring of liver voxels 2–6 voxels outside the lesion, divided by the standard deviation of that ring. Over the 518 lesions in our training split, the median CNR is only 1.616 (range 0.022–5.373). Low contrast is therefore the typical case on LiTS, not a rare exception. Small, low-contrast lesions are where we expect a strong baseline to fail most often [[TBD: share of missed test lesions in the lowest CNR tertile and the <10 mm stratum for A0]].

## 1.2 Research trajectory

Early deep-learning methods for LiTS combined 2D and 3D context. H-DenseUNet, for example, used a 2D DenseUNet for intra-slice features and a 3D network for inter-slice aggregation \cite{li2018hdenseunet}. The self-configuring nnU-Net \cite{isensee2021nnunet} then showed that a carefully configured U-Net, rather than a new architecture, set the state of the art across many 3D tasks, including the LiTS evaluation of MICCAI 2018 \cite{bilic2023lits}.

Subsequent work replaced or augmented convolutions to model long-range context. Examples are Swin Transformer encoders (Swin UNETR \cite{hatamizadeh2022swinunetr}), large-kernel ConvNeXt-style blocks (MedNeXt \cite{roy2023mednext}), and state-space (Mamba) blocks (U-Mamba \cite{ma2024umamba}, MambaClinix \cite{bian2026mambaclinix}). Self-supervised and omni-supervised pretraining on large CT collections, such as the VoCo family \cite{wu2024voco,wu2024largescale}, now provides strong initializations for 3D encoders. However, a large-scale re-evaluation found that many reported gains over nnU-Net disappear under rigorous validation \cite{isensee2024revisited}.

Liver-tumor papers in this journal have mostly built 2D or 2.5D networks with attention, multi-scale fusion and boundary guidance \cite{qi2024addunet,hu2024msml,cheng2024egunetr,you2024pgcnet,wu2025tcanet,wang2025rioUNeXt,raghaw2025tmpednet}. A more recent line adds frequency information. MSFFE-Net appends a Fourier magnitude image to the network input \cite{ma2025msffe}. TexLiverNet filters skip features in the Fourier domain \cite{jiang2025texlivernet}. A third approach enhances wavelet sub-bands of encoder features for low-contrast abdominal tumors \cite{han2025frequency}. The common motivation is that lesion boundaries and small lesions live in the higher frequency bands that pooling and deep encoders tend to suppress.

## 1.3 Gaps

**Amplitude-based boundary cues scale with contrast.** Most explicit boundary cues are amplitude measures: image gradients, edge detectors applied to intensities or predictions \cite{cheng2024egunetr}, or the energy of high-frequency coefficients. For an ideal step edge, the gradient magnitude is proportional to the step height. Such a cue therefore weakens linearly as lesion–liver contrast falls, which is the regime identified in Section 1.1. Phase congruency (PC) \cite{kovesi1999phase} measures how well local Fourier components agree in phase. It is dimensionless, and $\mathrm{PC}(a\,x) = \mathrm{PC}(x)$ for any $a > 0$. In noise-free synthetic volumes, we confirmed that PC changes by at most $8.6\times10^{-3}$ for planar boundaries ($2.3\times10^{-2}$ for a sphere) when lesion contrast changes from 5 to 40 HU, while gradient magnitude scales linearly (Section 4.8, Fig. 3).

Contrast invariance does not imply sensitivity. Kovesi's noise compensation subtracts a threshold estimated from the response of the finest filter scale \cite{kovesi1999phase}. This threshold is set by the noise level, not by the lesion contrast. With 5 HU white noise, the threshold suppressed PC almost everywhere at CNR ≤ 1. This was measured with the untrained operator on HU intensities, not on network features (Section 4.8). A fixed, hand-tuned PC is thus not a ready remedy for low-contrast lesions. Whether a learnable PC computed on network features helps is an empirical question, and we treat it as one.

**Frequency modules are not conditioned on local content.** Existing frequency-aware segmentation networks either apply a fixed decomposition \cite{li2020wavecnn} or adapt the spectral operation per image or per feature map \cite{ma2025msffe,jiang2025texlivernet,han2025frequency}. None decides, voxel by voxel, which frequency band to emphasize based on an explicit, contrast-normalized measure of local structure. A lesion boundary, a vessel wall and homogeneous parenchyma may need different band weightings within the same patch.

**Evaluation protocols are not comparable.** Tumor Dice values reported on LiTS in this journal range from about 65% to 90% (Section 2.4, Table S1). Much of this spread reflects protocol rather than method. Several studies split data at the slice level, so neighboring slices of one patient appear in both training and test sets. Others evaluate only tumor-containing slices, and most do not state how Dice is aggregated. This matters: for one model and test set, global Dice was 85.72% and per-case Dice 73.63% \cite{you2024pgcnet}. Under one slice-level protocol, the proposed network exceeded nnU-Net by only 0.07 points (89.61% vs 89.54%) \cite{ma2025msffe}. Lesion-wise detection metrics, which matter most for small lesions \cite{kofler2023blob,maierhein2024metrics}, are rarely reported in these studies; most report only voxel-level overlap. In addition, the 3DIRCADb dataset, often used as an "external" test set, appears to overlap with the LiTS training data \cite{bilic2023lits}.

## 1.4 Contributions

We present PhASER-3D, a 3D liver-tumor segmentation network that adds Phase–Frequency Coherence Routing (PFCR) to a pretrained nnU-Net. We evaluate it under a protocol designed to make each claim attributable. Our contributions are:

1. **A contrast-normalized, content-conditioned frequency routing module.** PFCR computes 3D monogenic PC \cite{felsberg2001monogenic} on a learnable one-channel projection of each encoder skip feature. It uses three learnable log-Gabor scales and a learnable Kovesi noise multiplier. A per-voxel router, conditioned on PC and a tumor prior, reweights low-, mid- and high-frequency groups of single-level 3D Haar sub-bands. The module is an exact identity at initialization, so it does not perturb the VoCo-pretrained encoder \cite{wu2024largescale}. A margin-ranking loss asks PC to be higher on lesion boundaries than on lesion interiors and the peritumoral ring, without regressing PC onto a mask.

2. **A patient-level, per-case and lesion-wise evaluation protocol for LiTS.** We use a fixed 83/21/27 patient split. All model and post-processing choices use the 21 validation cases, final-epoch weights are reported without checkpoint selection, and the 27 test cases are evaluated once. We report per-case and global Dice, lesion-wise recall, precision and F1, false positives per scan, HD95 (95th-percentile Hausdorff distance) and NSD (normalized surface distance). Results are stratified by lesion diameter and by CNR tertiles frozen on training lesions. Statistics use multiple seeds, paired bootstrap confidence intervals and Wilcoxon signed-rank tests. All baselines are retrained in the same pipeline. A literature table (Table S1) documents why published LiTS numbers cannot be compared directly.

3. **A mechanism analysis that separates phase evidence from its confounds.** Our ablations isolate phase evidence from amplitude evidence (a gradient-magnitude gate), routing from gating, information content from added capacity (spatially shuffled PC), and the effect of pretraining. We complement them with synthetic and real-lesion probes of PC and gradient responses versus CNR, inspection of learned filters and route maps, inference-time interventions and controlled contrast degradation. We report negative findings, including the noise-driven abstention of the fixed PC operator described in Section 1.3.
